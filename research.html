Research

What happens when two nucleons overlap?

For a brief moment, two nucleons in a nucleus can come close enough to overlap. We knock them apart with high-energy electrons and catch both.

About one in five nucleons is, at any moment, part of such a short-range correlated pair: two nucleons with large, nearly opposite momenta. Which pairs form, and how that changes with momentum, maps the nucleon–nucleon force at its shortest distances. Neutron–proton pairs dominate near 400 MeV/c, where the tensor force rules; towards 800 MeV/c proton–proton pairs grow as the repulsive core takes over. Most recently we showed that the shell structure of a nucleus decides which of its nucleons pair up.

Key papers: PRL 113, 022501 · PLB 820, 136523 · Nature 654, 619

Is a proton inside a nucleus the same as a free one?

No. Its quarks behave differently, and correlated pairs may be the reason.

Deep inelastic scattering shows that the quark distributions of bound nucleons are depleted at large momentum fraction: the EMC effect, found in 1983 and still not fully explained. Its size tracks the number of correlated pairs in each nucleus, which suggests the change happens during these brief, dense overlaps. We test this on deuterium: by catching the spectator nucleon, we learn event by event how tightly the struck nucleon was bound.

Key papers: RMP 89, 045002 · Nature 566, 354 · EPJA 60, 201

How do gluons behave inside a nucleus?

Almost nothing is known about high-momentum gluons in nuclei. We will measure them with the J/ψ.

A J/ψ is a charm quark bound to its antiquark. Ordinary matter has almost no charm, so making one near threshold takes an exchange of gluons with the target. In an approved Hall D experiment, with Igor among its spokespersons, we will produce J/ψ on helium-4 at and below the free-proton threshold, where only nucleons moving fast inside the nucleus, often in correlated pairs, can make the reaction happen.

Key papers: E12-25-002

Where are the quarks inside a proton?

We map quarks by position and momentum at the same time.

Elastic scattering shows where the charge sits inside a nucleon; deep inelastic scattering shows how its quarks share its momentum. Exclusive reactions, in which the electron produces a single photon or meson and leaves the nucleon intact, join the two into a three-dimensional picture. Our work includes deeply virtual Compton scattering on the neutron, and a test of the factorization that this picture relies on.

Key papers: Nature Phys. 16, 191 · arXiv:2103.00611

What will a collider show that fixed targets cannot?

At a collider, the slow nucleons we struggle to catch today fly out fast and can be measured directly.

The Electron-Ion Collider, now being built at Brookhaven National Laboratory, will collide electrons with protons and nuclei to image the quarks and gluons inside them. Spectator and recoiling nucleons, slow and hard to detect in a fixed-target experiment, emerge boosted along the beam into detectors close to it. We help design the detector and these measurements, from the ECCE concept to the ePIC experiment.

Key papers: PRC 105, 034001 · NIM A 1073, 170240

Where do we take data?

At Jefferson Lab's CEBAF accelerator, whose electron beams of up to 12 GeV feed four experimental halls.

Our experiments run in all four: the high-resolution spectrometers of Hall A; CLAS12 with the BAND neutron detector in Hall B; the HMS and SHMS spectrometers and the LAD detector in Hall C; and GlueX in Hall D.
